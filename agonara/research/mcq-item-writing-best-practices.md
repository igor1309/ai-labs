# Multiple-Choice Item-Writing Best Practices

Evidence-based guidance for authoring high-quality single-select questions,
anchored to Haladyna/Downing/Rodriguez (2002), Rodriguez (2005), the NBME
Item-Writing Guide, and the psychometrics literature. This feeds the
distractor-quality bar that **both** the curator (authoring program checks) and
the student (generating practice quizzes from accepted materials) must meet — the
quality requirements are intentionally the same for both consumers.

## Agonara project decisions

These override the literature default where noted; the rest of the report is the
faithful findings.

1. **Option count: 3–4, varied per item — not a fixed 3.** Rodriguez (2005)
   shows three options are psychometrically optimal *on average*, but Agonara
   deliberately does **not** pin every item to three. Author/generate **3 or 4
   options and vary the count across the bank**, keeping only options that
   function as real distractors. Varying the count also removes the count itself
   as a predictable structural cue.
2. **Shared bar, both roles.** The same quality criteria apply to curator-authored
   program checks and to student-side practice quizzes. The item-quality rules are
   not curator-only.
3. **Answer-position is a first-class rule.** The key's position must not be
   predictable across the bank (DoD #1). See Finding 1.

## Question

What are evidence-based best practices for writing high-quality multiple-choice /
single-select assessment questions, specifically addressing: (1) position of the
correct answer, (2) cueing via formatting and wording, (3) distractor quality, and
(4) other high-impact item-writing rules?

## Answer

The literature converges on a small set of high-impact, empirically grounded
rules: **write plausible, misconception-based distractors** (not arbitrary wrong
facts); **use few well-constructed options** (3 is the literature optimum; Agonara
uses 3–4 varied); **avoid "all/none of the above"**; **randomize option order
programmatically rather than manually balancing the key**; and **make the correct
option the same length and register as its distractors**.

## Findings

### 1. Position of the correct answer

**1a. Item writers and test-takers show a systematic middle-position bias on
isolated items.** Both favor middle over extreme positions by up to 3–4 to 1 on
isolated items. On assembled tests writers deliberately balance keys, which mostly
neutralizes bias in the key — but test-takers still guess toward the middle more
than a balanced key warrants.
- Attali, Y., & Bar-Hillel, M. (2003). "Guess Where: The Position of Correct
  Answers in Multiple-Choice Test Items as a Psychometric Variable." *Journal of
  Educational Measurement*, 40(2), 109–128.
  https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1745-3984.2003.tb01099.x
- Verified from search results citing the paper directly. Confidence: high.

**1b. Items with the key at position A are measurably easier than at position E.**
National exam study (n = 195,715): position A 52.3% vs. position E 48.1% accuracy
(4.2 pp; OR 1.18). Effect small (Cramér's V < .10) but compounds across a test.
- Pardo-García, C., et al. (2023). "Position of Correct Option and Distractors
  Impacts Responses to Multiple-Choice Items." PMC10470158.
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10470158/
- Verified from full page fetch. Confidence: high for the effect; medium for
  generalizability beyond this exam.

**1c. The strongest distractors cluster at middle positions; the weakest at the
last option.** 8,800 items / 110 tests, large effect sizes (best-distractor
position ηp² = .316; non-functioning distractor position ηp² = .284). Mechanism
likely reciprocal: test-takers gravitate to the middle; writers place stronger
distractors there.
- Pardo-García, C., & Barac-Nieto, M. (2021). "The Position of Distractors in
  Multiple-Choice Test Items: The Strongest Precede the Weakest." *Frontiers in
  Education*. https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2021.731763/full
- Verified from full page fetch. Confidence: high for the pattern; causal
  mechanism inferred.

**Recommendation:** Randomize option order at assembly. Avoid manual
key-balancing — it signals position-switching strategies to sophisticated
test-takers. Haladyna et al. (2002): "vary the position of the correct response
across items."
- Haladyna, T.M., Downing, S.M., & Rodriguez, M.C. (2002). "A Review of
  Multiple-Choice Item-Writing Guidelines for Classroom Assessment." *Applied
  Measurement in Education*, 15(3), 309–334.
  https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1207/S15324818AME1503_5

### 2. Cueing via formatting and wording

**2a. The correct option is disproportionately the longest option — the most
common inadvertent cue.** Writers add qualifications and completeness to the key
they do not apply to distractors. Documented in ~10–16% of audited items with
non-functioning distractors.
- Pande, S.S., et al. (2011). "A practical discussion to avoid common pitfalls
  when constructing multiple-choice question items." PMC3045096.
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3045096/
- Corroborated: Abdulghani, H.M., et al. (2013). PMC3809311.
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3809311/ ; NBME Item-Writing Guide
  (6th ed., 2021). https://www.nbme.org/educators/item-writing-guide
- Verified across three independent sources. Confidence: high.

**2b. Grammatical agreement is a cue.** Distractors that don't follow
grammatically from the stem can be eliminated without content knowledge.
- PMC3045096; PMC3809311. Verified from two sources. Confidence: high for the
  rule; effect size not established.

**2c. Absolute terms ("always," "never," "only") flag options as likely wrong;
hedged language flags them as likely correct.**
- PMC3045096; PMC3809311; NBME guide. Verified across three sources. Confidence:
  high for the rule; moderate on quantified effect.

**2d. Vague frequency terms ("often," "usually," "rarely") add
construct-irrelevant variance** — not defined consistently even by experts.
- PMC3045096. Confidence: moderate — single source chain.

**2e. Word repetition between stem and an option cues that option.**
- PMC3045096; PMC3809311. Verified from two sources.

**2f. Convergence strategy:** the key tends to share the most elements with the
other options; test-takers learn to exploit this.
- PMC3045096. Confidence: moderate — one source; conventional in item-writing
  training.

**"Make options homogeneous" (synthesis).** Options should be parallel in
grammar, similar in length, from the same content category, and consistent in
register/terminology. Appears in Haladyna et al. (2002), the NBME guide, and all
three PMC sources. It is the structural principle underlying 2a–2f.

### 3. Distractor quality

**3a. The most prevalent driver of non-functioning distractors is low cognitive
level, not poor wording.** Study of 181 questions: 31.6% of distractors
non-functional (<5% selection); leading cause was low-cognitive-level (recall)
items (40% of flaw instances); "limited plausible / irrelevant distractors" 16%.
- Gómez-Galán, J., et al. (2020). "Nonfunctional distractor analysis." PMC7372664.
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7372664/
- Verified from full page fetch. Confidence: high for the breakdown; moderate
  generalizability.

**3b. Distractors should represent named, plausible misconceptions or predictable
errors — not arbitrary wrong facts or joke options.** Unanimous across sources;
implausible distractors are never selected and add no discrimination.
- NBME guide; Haladyna et al. (2002); PMC3045096; PMC7372664. Strong consensus.

**3c. Avoid "all of the above" / "none of the above."** "All of the above" lets
partial knowledge pass; "none of the above" tests only that distractors are wrong.
Both penalize slow readers.
- Haladyna et al. (2002); PMC3045096; PMC3809311; NBME guide. Abdulghani et al.
  documented removal improving test quality across cohorts (67% → 21% flaw rate).
  Confidence: high.

**3d. Three options are empirically optimal for most purposes (Agonara uses 3–4,
varied — see project decisions).** Rodriguez (2005) meta-analysis, 80 years of
research: cutting 5→3 options slightly lowers difficulty but does not reduce
reliability/discrimination, and allows more items per testing session. *Random*
option removal is harmful; removing *non-functioning* distractors is not.
- Rodriguez, M.C. (2005). "Three Options Are Optimal for Multiple-Choice Items: A
  Meta-Analysis of 80 Years of Research." *Educational Measurement: Issues and
  Practice*, 24(2), 3–13.
  https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1745-3992.2005.00006.x
- Verified from search snippet + secondary summary. Confidence: very high for the
  general conclusion; the result assumes removed options were non-functioning.

**3e. Near-duplicate or overlapping options are a structural flaw** — enable
logical elimination and waste option slots.
- NBME guide; PMC7372664 (repeated distractors 1.7% of NFD flaws). Verified from
  two sources.

### 4. Other high-impact rules

**4a. Avoid negative stems ("NOT", "EXCEPT"); if used, make the negation salient.**
- NBME guide; Haladyna et al. (2002); PMC3045096. Verified from three sources.

**4b. The stem should pass the "cover-the-options" test** — a knowledgeable
examinee can answer from the stem alone before seeing options.
- NBME guide; Haladyna et al. (2002). Consensus across major guides.

**4c. Test application/reasoning, not isolated recognition** — low-cognitive-level
items are the dominant cause of non-functioning distractors (see 3a).
- NBME guide; PMC7372664.

**4d. Items should not cue each other (local independence).**
- Haladyna et al. (2002). Principle is established measurement theory; no
  prevalence study fetched.

**4e. Numeric/date options should be ordered logically** (ascending/descending).
- PMC3045096; NBME guide.

## Synthesis for Agonara's distractor-quality bar

1. **Write to misconceptions, not to arbitrary wrongness.** Each distractor must
   be nameable — what a learner believes under a specific, common error.
2. **Use 3–4 options, varied across the bank** (Agonara decision over the strict-3
   literature default), keeping only functioning distractors.
3. **Eliminate "all/none of the above" categorically.**
4. **Audit option length first.** Flag items where the key is >30% longer than the
   mean distractor length.
5. **Make options homogeneous in category and register.**
6. **Randomize option order; don't let the key's position be predictable across
   the bank.**

## Confidence and gaps

**Solidly established (multiple sources + data):** three-option optimality
(Rodriguez 2005); middle-position bias (Attali & Bar-Hillel 2003; Frontiers 2021;
PMC 2023); non-functioning-distractor prevalence and primary cause (PMC7372664);
all/none-of-the-above as a validity threat; longer-key cue; grammatical-agreement
cue.

**Strong consensus but no isolated effect-size data:** absolute-term detection;
negative-stem penalty; inter-item clueing.

**Source nuances:** PMC 2023 found position A easiest; Attali & Bar-Hillel found
middle items easier in isolated contexts — not contradictory (different
measurement conditions), and the practical guidance (randomize) is the same. Three
vs. four options: NBME and some high-stakes programs retain four; Rodriguez (2005)
argues three. Agonara's 3–4 varied choice is a deliberate middle position.

**Could not verify from fetched sources:** the verbatim Haladyna et al. (2002)
31-rule table (PDF unreadable; cited rules corroborated by independent empirical
sources but without exact rule numbers); the NBME guide PDF full text (timed out;
NBME content here is from the website page and a readkong.com mirror — treat as
high-confidence summary, not verbatim).
