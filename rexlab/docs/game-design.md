# Game design questions

Answer these for each level of a game before writing its Gherkin. They
come from research on games for 5-7-year-olds and on care games children
return to (LAB-387). A level that cannot answer them well is a quiz, not
a game: the care game's need-to-item rounds and its Option A prototype
(LAB-281) both failed here.

| Question | A good answer |
|---|---|
| **Goal:** what does the child want, in their own words, and how is it shown without text? | "Make the baby big", shown by an egg or a hungry face, not a label |
| **First tap:** what happens within 0.1 s? | Motion and sound, on anything tappable |
| **Choice:** what real choice does each tap make? | Two or more plausible options with different outcomes, not one obvious tap |
| **Being wrong:** what happens after a wrong choice? | A funny reaction that shows why, then another try at once with nothing lost |
| **Character:** how does the creature react to what the child does? | Feelings tied to the action: it crunches, spits out, burps, snores |
| **Surprise:** what can the child not predict? | A hidden or random thing each round |
| **Reward:** what is the round's pay-off? | New things to play with (a creature joins, it grows, a fact), not a star or a score |
| **Replay:** what changes next time? | Random content, plus something hidden to find |
| **Across sessions:** what carries over? | A visible collection or something that grows |
| **Round:** how long is it, and how is progress shown? | 30-90 seconds, progress shown as things, not numbers |
| **Complexity:** where does it live, and how much is held in mind? | One new thing to reason about per level, never speed or a timer; 2-3 items for Hatchling, at most 4 for Paleontologist |
| **Layout:** does it change across levels? | No: content and rules change, the controls stay put, and the bottom edge stays clear for resting wrists |
| **Babyish:** would a 7-year-old call it "for babies"? | Real names and facts at the higher levels |
| **Toy test:** is it fun with the rules ignored? | Tapping around still makes things happen |

## What to avoid

- One obvious tap per screen, or a meter that fills whatever the child does.
- The same "great!" for everything, and silence after a mistake.
- Stickers, stars or hearts promised for each round as the reason to play:
  they lower a child's later interest. Feedback that shows what the child
  did does the opposite.
- Rules in text, long intros, and waiting.

## Checking a level

A playtest with the child answers what this table cannot. Watch whether
they start another round unasked, say what they were trying to do, and
notice the reward. A level the child calls boring is redesigned against
these questions, not made harder.
