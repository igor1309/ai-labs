# AI For Work Course Selling Points

## Core Selling Proposition

The course sells a system for non-developers to delegate information-work routines to AI agents without losing accuracy or control. Its main contrast is not "learn AI faster" but "stop manually moving data between dozens of tabs and build your own durable automations against your real work — without writing code and without depending on any one tool."

The course explicitly refuses two adjacent positionings: developer-style "vibe-coding" of apps, and the no-code/low-code promise of plug-and-play turnkey automations. The differentiation lives in that double refusal.

## Primary Selling Points

### Built for Non-Developers, Not Engineers

The page explicitly targets information workers in marketing, research, sales, operations, HR, analytics, and management — not developers. This makes the offer credible for buyers who feel that AI-coding content is not aimed at them.

### "Delegate Routine, Keep Expertise"

The framing is that AI does the routine; the participant keeps the judgment. This addresses the deeper anxiety that learning AI is somehow a step toward replacing oneself.

### Universal Task Structure (Tool-Independent Skill)

The course teaches a single recurring structure: task → input data → result → verification. This is the explicit answer to "tools change every month, so why bother learning one?" — the skill is positioned as the structure, not the tool.

Direct phrasing: "Навык: как ставить задачи агенту, давать контекст, проверять результат" (the skill is task-framing, context-giving, result-verification).

### Build Your Own, Not Receive a Template

Participants build their own automations against their own work data. The page argues this is what makes the skill survive past the course — and it is the contrast against turnkey-template marketing.

### Real Work as the Final Project

The capstone is each participant's own real work routine, automated and presented. The promise is that the participant leaves with at least one deployed automation they will keep using.

### Four Concrete Modules with Concrete Outputs

Each module names a deliverable a non-technical buyer can picture: clickable report, open-source research, closed-source research, browser automation. This lowers buyer uncertainty about what will actually be produced.

### Light Time Commitment

Roughly 30-40 minutes of weekly homework, plus live sessions. The page makes the commitment look compatible with a full-time non-engineering job.

### Hand-Holding Onboarding

A dedicated onboarding week walks through subscriptions, installation, payment, and the first execution. This neutralizes the "I will be lost from day one" objection for non-technical buyers.

### Anti-Hype Stance

The course explicitly distances itself from "vibe-coding apps" («Не будем вайбкодить приложения») and from low-code/no-code framing. This signals seriousness to buyers who have been burned by hype.

### Verification-First Quality Discipline

The page emphasizes detecting agent errors and checking sources rather than promising flawless output. This reframes AI risk as a designable verification step, not a brand of model.

### Practical Authoring Pair

Two complementary instructors — a technical/case-study founder and a course-design/community founder — covers both "is this real" and "will I actually finish" buyer concerns.

### Three Buying Tiers

Self-serve, with-feedback, and VIP tiers let buyers pick their level of high-touch support. Higher tiers are seat-limited (30 / 10), creating scarcity and matching buyers who want direct project review.

### Risk Mitigation

Full refund within the first week neutralizes the largest single objection from non-technical buyers who are unsure whether they will keep up.

### Career-Relevance Signal

The page argues that AI skills are now appearing in non-developer job postings («навыки работы с ИИ приходят в вакансии за пределами разработки»). This frames the course as career leverage for information workers, not as a hobby.

## Pain Points Addressed

### "I Move Information Between Dozens of Tabs by Hand"

The course responds with the data-processing module ("from messy table to presentation") and browser automation.

### "I'm Afraid of Complex Files and Folders"

The course responds with structured task framing and a hand-held onboarding week to lower the entry threshold.

### "Research Eats My Whole Day"

The course responds with the open-source and closed-source research modules, both of which produce structured outputs.

### "I Don't Know Where to Start with AI"

The course responds with the universal task structure and a single onboarding path.

### "Too Many Tools and Subscriptions"

The course responds by teaching the structure, not the product, and by walking through subscription/installation choices once.

### "I'm Scared the AI Will Be Wrong and I Won't Notice"

The course responds with verification-first discipline: every workflow ends in checking sources before acting.

## Buyer Personas

### Information-Heavy Operator

Spends most of the day moving data between systems. The best message is: stop hand-transferring information, build a routine that runs once and verify the output.

### Researcher / Analyst

Lives in open- and closed-source research. The best message is: agents collect and structure; you keep the judgment and the final read.

### Founder / Project Manager

Wants internal automations without hiring engineering. The best message is: build a few high-leverage routines yourself, no code, no waiting on a developer.

### HR / Recruiter

Sources, screens, monitors. The best message is: turn channel and job-board scraping into a repeatable hiring funnel you control end-to-end.

### Domain Expert Outside Tech

Holds the expertise, lacks technical confidence. The best message is: keep the expertise; delegate the routine; verify what the agent gives you.

## Objections and Responses

### "I Can't Code"

Response: No coding required. All work happens through text, files, tables, folders, and websites.

### "Tools Change Constantly — Why Learn One Now?"

Response: The course teaches a structure (task → input → result → verification) that transfers across tools, not button sequences on any one product.

### "I'll Just Get Templates I Can't Adapt"

Response: Participants build their own automations during the course; that is the explicit method.

### "I Might Not Keep Up"

Response: Roughly 30-40 minutes of homework per week, all sessions recorded, full refund within the first week.

### "What If the AI Is Wrong?"

Response: Every workflow ends with a verification step against sources. Detecting agent error is treated as part of the skill, not a separate concern.

### "Who Is This Not For?"

Response: The page is explicit — it is not for developers building applications, and not for buyers expecting plug-and-play turnkey automations.

## Strongest Conversion Claims

- Non-developers can build their own AI automations against real work data, without writing code.
- The skill (task framing, context, verification) survives changes in underlying tools.
- Each participant leaves with at least one deployed automation against their own routine, presented to peers.
- Verification-first discipline reduces the risk of acting on hallucinated agent output.
- The course is jointly designed and delivered by a technical AI practitioner and an experienced online-education producer.

## Comparison Notes vs Khakhalev (cross-reference)

Both courses share an anti-hype stance and a "system, not tricks" posture, but they sit in opposite halves of the AI-skills market and do not compete directly. Documenting the contrast here for the decision matrix:

- **Audience:** Khakhalev sells to mid/senior developers; AI For Work sells to non-developers in information-heavy roles.
- **Object of practice:** Khakhalev's spine is a shared Task Tracker codebase; AI For Work's spine is each participant's own real work routine.
- **Tool framing:** Khakhalev names specific tools (Codex, Claude Code) and rules out Cursor; AI For Work deliberately abstracts away from any one tool, teaching a universal task structure.
- **Quality discipline:** Khakhalev frames it as code review, security review, and entropy management; AI For Work frames it as source verification before acting on agent output.
- **Capstone:** Khakhalev — a Plan & Act workflow on the demo project; AI For Work — a personal-routine automation presented to peers.
- **Anti-positioning:** Khakhalev refuses "vibe coding"; AI For Work refuses both "vibe coding apps" and "low-code/no-code turnkey templates."
- **Pricing posture:** Khakhalev's tiers (30k / 50k / 80k ₽) target individual developers and employer-paid buyers; AI For Work's tiers (34k / 54k / 69k ₽ early bird, capped seats on the upper two) target individual professionals.
