# AI assistant landscape for psychologists and mental health practices in summer 2026

## Executive summary

As of June 2026, the market that is actually usable by psychologists, psychotherapists, counselors, behavioral health providers, and mental health clinics is no longer a vague “AI for therapists” category. It has separated into three practical lanes: specialist documentation startups, AI features embedded inside behavioral-health EHR/practice-management systems, and AI tools inside therapist networks/platforms. The most mature lane is still documentation: ambient scribing, transcript-to-note drafting, structured therapy-note generation, and treatment-plan drafting. The next-most mature lane is session prep, longitudinal client context, and measurement-based-care support. Supervision workflows, compliance QA, and between-session engagement are real but still uneven across vendors. citeturn18search1turn17search11turn15search11turn19search10turn8search14turn7search8turn36search2

The strongest evidence-backed specialist vendors in this market are Upheal, Mentalyc, Blueprint, Eleos, JotPsych, and Oasys. They all show more than a marketing page: public pricing or packaging, current product pages, recent launches or funding, current jobs/careers signals, and some combination of customer counts, case studies, partnerships, or documented organizational usage. Upheal says it supports 800+ organizations and raised a $10 million Series A in November 2024; Mentalyc says 30,000+ therapists use it and still publishes active product/careers content in 2026; Blueprint says it supports 70,000+ mental health professionals and is actively hiring; Eleos raised a $60 million Series C in January 2025 and reports 120+ healthcare organizations across 30+ states; JotPsych closed a $5 million seed in July 2025 and says its scribe has drafted 2.5+ million patient notes; Oasys raised $4.6 million in January 2026 and reported 25+ clinic partnerships, hundreds of providers, and thousands of patients. citeturn18search3turn18search4turn18search2turn18search12turn17search11turn17search9turn16search14turn15search4turn15search11turn15search3turn19search1turn19search10turn19search12turn20search0turn20search4turn20search2turn32search0turn32search4turn32search11

The most important change since 2024 is that incumbents are now real AI contenders instead of merely promising “AI soon.” SimplePractice launched and then expanded Note Taker into Care Aide in June 2026; TherapyNotes built out TherapyFuel across scribe, session assist, treatment plans, and secure-message note generation; Valant expanded AI Notes Assist in February 2026 to support 100+ behavioral-health templates; and TheraNest’s AI Session Assistant has rolled out in-person support, browser access, case summaries, and mobile access across 2026. These are no longer generic “AI” claims attached to legacy EHRs; they are concrete workflow features inside existing documentation flows. citeturn8search14turn8search3turn8search2turn8search10turn7search11turn7search8turn7search3turn7search17turn38search13turn38search0turn38search19turn36search2turn36search10turn36search17turn36search20

For buyers, the market is no longer about “which AI tool exists,” but about which deployment model fits the practice. Solo and small-group clinicians now have credible choices in standalone tools and AI-native EHRs. Larger clinics and enterprise behavioral health organizations have stronger options in Eleos, Valant, TheraNest/Ensora, TherapyNotes, and Oasys, where documentation, compliance, and operational workflows connect more directly to reimbursement and audit readiness. citeturn18search15turn18search1turn19search10turn38search21turn37search1turn23search10turn20search22

A practical caution: many vendors now sound similar on websites. The real differentiators in summer 2026 are not “uses AI” or “is HIPAA compliant,” but whether the product has a real consent flow, a clear BAA path, explicit data-retention terms, structured note-format support, live or uploaded audio workflows, treatment-plan continuity, and actual clinical or operational proof of use. ## Product deep dives

**Upheal.** Upheal is now best understood as an AI-native mental-health EHR rather than only an AI note tool. Its public packaging bundles AI documentation, scheduling, practice forms, telehealth, client billing, messaging, compliance checking, migration, and a beta AI assistant into one workflow. Pricing is unusually transparent for the category: $1 per completed session capped at $69 per month, with no per-provider base fee. Privacy/compliance signals are relatively strong for a startup: BAA, SOC 2 messaging, HIPAA positioning, and a public security page. Active use evidence is stronger than most peers because the company publicly names organizational customers and says it supports 800+ organizations. Development velocity is also obvious: Series A funding in late 2024, current API docs, current messaging product pages, and live hiring. The wedge versus incumbents is “replace the practice stack,” not merely “write notes faster.” The main diligence question is whether every non-note module is good enough to replace an incumbent EHR in the buyer’s specific workflow. citeturn18search1turn18search5turn18search3turn18search4turn18search10turn18search13turn18search2

**Mentalyc.** Mentalyc remains one of the clearest therapist-first tools in the market. Its product scope now spans AI note taking, treatment planning, progress tracking, and therapeutic-alliance insights, with support for common therapy formats including SOAP, DAP, BIRP, GIRP, PIE, intake, progress, and supervision/consultation notes. The company explicitly positions itself as a complement to existing EHRs, with EHR sync/copy workflows rather than a full admin stack. Its strongest market signal is category recognition and breadth of therapy-specific workflows; its weakest is that many traction claims are still self-reported. The company says 30,000+ therapists use it, and its public history page shows a move from basic note automation into clinical-intelligence features through 2025. The product is still visibly alive in 2026 via active content and a public careers page. It is a strong candidate for solo and small-group practices that do not want to migrate their whole EHR. The two issues to diligence are independent proof of traction and how robust its compliance/retention settings are for insurance-heavy or enterprise use. citeturn17search11turn33search15turn17search9turn16search14

**Blueprint.** Blueprint started in measurement-based care and has expanded into an AI-assisted EHR and clinician assistant. That origin still matters. Among the vendors reviewed, Blueprint has the clearest MBC DNA: suggested assessments, worksheet assignment, session prep, after-visit summaries, and treatment-plan drafting sit alongside notes rather than appearing as disconnected extra features. Its public pricing is also unusually buyer-friendly: free EHR, assistant starting at $0.99 per session. Blueprint says it supports 70,000+ licensed therapists and hundreds of thousands of sessions per month; those are self-reported but materially stronger than the evidence available for many smaller competitors. The company is still hiring, still publishing product/legal/AI content in 2026, and continues to stress assistive rather than autonomous use. Blueprint is one of the best options when the practice wants documentation plus outcomes tracking, prep, and structured interventions in a single environment. The main limitation is that its strongest fit remains outpatient therapy workflows rather than heavy enterprise QA/compliance operations. citeturn15search4turn15search11turn15search9turn15search3turn15search1

**Eleos.** Eleos is the most mature enterprise behavioral-health AI company in this set. It is not just a note scribe. Its platform combines documentation, session intelligence, care-ops automation, compliance-risk detection, and now broader AI agents across the care journey. The strongest traction signal is organizational scale: 120+ healthcare organizations across 30+ states, plus public case studies showing large documentation-time reductions and faster submission cycles. The strongest development signal is the January 2025 $60 million Series C, which pushed total funding above $120 million, followed by an April 2026 product expansion into AI agents. Security posture is also enterprise-grade on paper, with HITRUST, SOC 2 Type II, ISO 27001, ISO 27799, and ISO 42001 listed. The wedge versus note-taking startups is compliance, prevention of denials, and operational oversight. For community mental health, payer-heavy environments, and larger clinics that need QA/compliance dashboards, Eleos belongs on the very short list. For a solo psychologist, it is often more platform than needed. citeturn19search10turn19search12turn19search15turn19search19turn19search1turn19search4

**JotPsych.** JotPsych is one of the clearest “young company with real product velocity” cases. It started from behavioral-health scribing and is expanding into a broader, reimbursement-oriented operating layer: billing intelligence, compliance audits, e-prescribing, integrated measures, and practice management. Its funding story is clean and recent: a $5 million seed led by Base10 Partners in July 2025. The company says its scribe has drafted more than 2.5 million patient notes, which is a far stronger workflow signal than generic “AI-powered” marketing copy. Its specialty positioning is also sharper than many competitors: psychiatry/behavioral-health-specific templates, treatment plans, integrated PHQ-9/GAD-7/PCL-5/MDQ, and an explicit audit-ready/reimbursement narrative. Public modular pricing is a plus. The risk is classic early-stage breadth creep: the roadmap from scribe into full agentic EHR is ambitious, so buyers should test whether today’s non-scribe modules are production-grade or still in transition. citeturn20search0turn20search4turn20search18turn20search15turn20search1turn20search17turn20search12

**SimplePractice Care Aide / Note Taker.** SimplePractice now has real AI, not just a placeholder roadmap. Note Taker is live, and Care Aide expands it into session prep, telehealth agenda help, treatment planning beta, and client summaries. This matters because SimplePractice already has one of the deepest installed bases in private-practice behavioral health. The product is tightly embedded: transcript-in-session, note draft immediately afterward, and chart-aware prep summaries. Public pricing is also valuable for buyers: Note Taker is a monthly add-on, while Care Aide bundles the broader AI suite. The strongest development evidence is the June 2026 launch of Care Aide and the support-document updates around transcript retention on June 17–19, 2026. The most important caveat is governance: starting in June 2026, SimplePractice may retain de-identified, de-coupled transcripts for improvement unless preferences are adjusted. Buyers who are very strict on data minimization should review that closely. For existing SimplePractice practices, though, this is now a serious default shortlist option. citeturn8search14turn8search3turn8search5turn8search2turn8search7turn8search8turn8search10

**TherapyNotes TherapyFuel.** TherapyNotes has built one of the most credible incumbent AI suites in behavioral health. TherapyFuel is no longer just note drafting; it includes Scribe, Session Assist, treatment-plan generation, and even note generation from secure messages. Its workflow is particularly strong for practices that already live inside TherapyNotes because the AI output lands in the same documentation system, with mobile support added in early 2026. Public pricing is straightforward: TherapyFuel is a per-clinician add-on. TherapyNotes is a conservative, documentation-centric environment, and that shows in the feature set: strong progress-note support, pre-session summaries, and treatment-plan linkage rather than flashy “copilot” branding. The main limitation is that the value is mostly captive to existing TherapyNotes users; it is less attractive as a separate AI layer than as an incumbent-upgrade path. But for current TherapyNotes practices, it deserves to be treated as one of the market’s most realistic production options rather than as a lagging legacy vendor. citeturn7search11turn7search8turn7search3turn7search17turn7search0turn7search4

**Valant AI Notes Assist.** Valant’s AI Notes Assist is one of the better examples of an incumbent doing more than generic transcription. ## Practical conclusions for psychologist or clinic

The mature category today is AI documentation. That includes ambient scribing, upload-to-note workflows, structured progress-note drafting, and increasingly treatment-plan drafting inside the same charting flow. This category is no longer experimental. It is live across startups and incumbents: Upheal, Mentalyc, Blueprint, JotPsych, SimplePractice, TherapyNotes, Valant, TheraNest, TherapyAppointment, Owl Practice, and Grow all have concrete note-generation workflows in production. citeturn18search1turn17search11turn15search11turn20search18turn8search14turn7search11turn38search13turn36search2turn27search11turn28search11turn39search4

The category that is promising but still uneven is longitudinal clinical intelligence: session prep, case summaries, continuity across sessions, MBC, and compliance/QA. Blueprint is strongest on MBC and prep. Eleos is strongest on enterprise QA/compliance/care-ops. Mentalyc, JotPsych, Oasys, and TherapyNotes/Valant are building more continuity across notes and plans, but the real-world quality of these second-order features still needs buyer-side testing rather than trust in demos. citeturn15search11turn19search4turn17search11turn20search18turn32search13turn7search8turn38search7

For a **solo psychologist**, the first products to examine are Upheal, Mentalyc, SimplePractice Care Aide if already on SimplePractice, TherapyNotes TherapyFuel if already on TherapyNotes, and possibly TherapyAppointment or Owl Practice if workflow/geography matches. Upheal and Mentalyc are the strongest independent specialist options; SimplePractice and TherapyNotes are the easiest low-friction upgrades for existing users; Owl Practice is especially strong for Canada. citeturn18search5turn17search11turn8search2turn7search0turn27search23turn28search4

For a **group practice or clinic**, the first products to examine are Eleos, Valant AI Notes Assist, TheraNest AI Session Assistant, Upheal, JotPsych, and Oasys. The common pattern is that these products go beyond a single clinician’s note and start addressing coding, compliance, audits, care coordination, team configuration, billing, or operational standardization. Eleos is strongest for larger, payer-sensitive organizations; Valant and TheraNest are strongest when the practice is already on those EHRs; Upheal and JotPsych are stronger if the clinic is willing to adopt a challenger platform; Oasys is promising for practices that want measurement + operational automation together. citeturn19search10turn38search13turn36search2turn18search15turn20search22turn32search13

For **training, supervision, and QA**, the strongest practical bets are Valant, TheraNest, Eleos, and—at smaller scale—TherapyNotes and Owl Practice. Valant and Eleos have the clearest connection to audit/compliance and review workflows. TheraNest publicly mentions supervision, handoffs, and case summaries. Owl’s fit is especially notable for Canadian academic/student-wellness settings. citeturn38search19turn19search11turn36search2turn28search8turn7search8

Before buying, the vendor questions that matter most are operational, not abstract. Ask exactly which note types are native today; whether notes are generated from ambient audio, uploaded recordings, dictated recap, typed summary, or all four; whether treatment plans, intake notes, discharge summaries, and supervision notes are supported; how data retention works for raw audio, transcript, and de-identified derivatives; whether a BAA is signed by default or only for certain plans; whether customer data is ever used for model improvement and how opt-out works; which EHR integrations are native vs. copy/paste; whether the product supports measurement-based care or only note generation; and whether the organization can export all notes, transcripts, and metadata if it leaves. These are the questions that separate real clinical infrastructure from a generic LLM wrapper with a therapy skin. citeturn8search8turn10search0turn4search2turn22search7turn27search17turn18search10turn38search13turn15search11
