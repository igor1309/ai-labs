# Solo Practitioner Pains

## 1. Holding the thread across sessions

Before and between sessions, the practitioner must reconstruct where each client is: prior goals, what was tried, homework set, risks flagged, threads left unresolved. With a full caseload and no team, supervisor, or shared chart, this memory work is entirely manual and entirely theirs — the practitioner *is* the continuity system.

## 2. Turning each session into a usable note

Every session must become a written clinical record in an accepted format (SOAP, DAP, BIRP, intake, discharge, etc.). This is unpaid post-session labor that lands on the same person who just did the draining clinical hour, and even a drafted note still has to be checked and corrected — omissions, unsupported claims, errors, risky wording — before it can be signed.

## 3. Carrying the data-trust burden alone

The practitioner is the sole party responsible for whether client data is safe: consent, retention, deletion, what happens to recordings, jurisdictional limits. There is no IT or legal function to delegate this to, so the responsibility and the "what happens to my data?" friction land entirely on one person.

## 4. Being the entire back office

The practitioner is simultaneously clinician, scheduler, biller, and administrator. Running a one-person practice across separate, disconnected tools multiplies overhead on the same person who is also doing the clinical work.

## 5. Tracking whether the client is improving

The practitioner has no built-in feedback loop on whether the treatment is working. Outcomes and measures sit outside the session in scattered forms, so progress is judged by memory and impression rather than tracked signal. And collecting a score is only half of it: the harder part is turning that measure into an actual care action — a worksheet, a follow-up, a plan change — rather than a number that changes nothing.

## 6. Keeping the treatment plan linked to the sessions

The treatment plan is the clinical spine — problem → goal → intervention → client response → progress → next step — and it has to stay current with what actually happens session to session. Keeping that thread intact (mapping interventions, tagging progress, generating the next step) is ongoing manual upkeep that drifts the moment it is not maintained.

## 7. Producing between-session client materials

Clients leave with things to use between sessions — homework, CBT thought records, DBT skills worksheets, exposure plans, mood and sleep diaries, plain-language psychoeducation — each adapted to the client's age, language, and cognitive style. Creating and tailoring these is recurring clinical work the practitioner does alone, on top of the session and the note.

## 8. Making the record defensible for insurance and audit

For a practitioner who takes insurance, the clinical record has to survive payer scrutiny, audit, and denial — documentation that proves medical necessity and supports the claim. The accountability for getting it right sits entirely with the practitioner.

## 9. In-room friction from recording and AI

Bringing a recording device or an AI scribe into the session can change the room: the client may hold back or self-censor, and the therapeutic alliance can suffer. The practitioner has to weigh the documentation benefit against the cost to the relationship.

## 10. Developing skills in isolation

A solo has no supervisor or peers on hand to rehearse difficult interventions, review their own sessions against a modality, or practice with hard cases. Maintaining and growing clinical skill falls on the practitioner alone.
